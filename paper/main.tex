\begin{abstract}
Closed-form continuous-time (CfC) networks compute a nonlinear recurrence, so their cost is
$\Theta(L)$ sequential steps. The natural research move is to make most of the state cheap: permute channels
into a small \emph{hot} set that keeps the full CfC update and a large \emph{cold} set that is linearised and
advanced by a parallel associative scan. This report builds that architecture (\piCfC{}) and, more
importantly, measures it against the baseline it is supposed to replace. Four results stand out.
(i)~The underlying algebraic claim is \emph{exact}: a simultaneous permutation of the gate parameters,
the recurrent tensor and the readout is a bilateral isomorphism verified to $1.0\times10^{-15}$ in fp64 and
$4.6\times10^{-7}$ in fp32, while the one-sided variant that a build-time fusion would produce fails by
$0.98$--$1.08$ relative error. (ii)~The \emph{accuracy} claim is conditional, not free: the hot fraction
$\rho$ must be large enough for the task's state complexity --- $\rho=1/8$ suffices for parity
(accuracy $1.000$ after five epochs of decoupling-aware fine-tuning, versus $0.505$ zero-shot) and for the
flip-flop automaton ($1.000$ zero-shot at $\rho=1/16$), but a modulo-3 counter needs $\rho\ge1/4$, and a
limit-cycle oscillator with spectral radius $\approx1$ loses its $R^2$ until the readout is recalibrated
($-0.22\to+0.26$). (iii)~The \emph{speed} claim holds only in a kernel-limited regime that we map exactly. Over the
60-cell sweep the best decoupled configuration of each shape is $8.6$--$86.5\times$ faster than a
sequential PyTorch loop and $7.3$--$45.4\times$ faster than the strongest sequential baseline we could
construct --- the minimum of the loop and a \emph{single-launch} Triton kernel that runs all $L$ steps in
one program per batch row, so that no speedup is bought with launch overhead that a reviewer would simply
remove. At the operating point the accuracy study recommends ($\rho=1/4$) the gain over that baseline is
$5.6$--$7.0\times$ at $D=256$, $7.2$--$8.3\times$ at $D=512$, $1.8$--$3.2\times$ at $D=1024$ and
$2.6$--$2.8\times$ at $D=2048$; a single-launch baseline is not a cure for a bandwidth-bound recurrence,
and we say so with the measurement, because at $D=1024$ it is $3.6\times$ \emph{slower} than the loop it
replaces. Tensor cores buy $1$--$2\%$ and cost a factor of $60$ in accuracy (TF32 pushes the trajectory
error to $2\times10^{-5}$), so every gated row stays IEEE fp32. On 16 pinned Zen~5 cores our own AVX-512
sequential kernel is already $\sim15\times$ faster than the PyTorch loop; merging the four stages of the
decoupled path into one C entry point makes $\pi$-CfC win 56 of 60 sweep cells (up to $26\times$) against
that kernel. (iv)~The effect is not an artefact of our generators: on real WikiText bytes and on ten real
CIF video clips the Jacobian ordering cuts the zero-shot decoupling penalty at $\rho=1/4$ from $+0.81$ to
$+0.53$ bits per byte ($+0.29$ with one Picard step) and from $-0.65$ to $-0.02$ in $R^2$; five epochs of
hot-only fine-tuning recover the byte model to $2.923$ bits per byte against $2.882$ for the exact model on
the same held-out region, and the closed-form readout recalibration rescues a collapsed pure-linear video
model from $-2.71$ to $0.831$ in $R^2$. On those streams the same kernels deliver $6.8\times$ and $3.9\times$
at $\rho=1/4$ ($11.9\times$ and $4.2\times$ at $\rho=1/8$) over the strongest sequential baseline.
Three contiguous, disjoint regions (70/15/15, split inside every clip) separate training, ranking and
scoring, so no approximation in this report is measured on data it was fitted to. Every number here comes from a logged run with a numerical gate ($<4.4\times10^{-7}$
against an fp32 reference, including the single-launch baseline at every width) and an in-process RSS
probe (peak \code{RssAnon} 1\,009~MB for the model runs; 1\,573~MB for the heaviest benchmark cell, an
overshoot we report rather than hide).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A CfC layer propagates
\begin{equation}
h_t = a_t \odot h_{t-1} + (1-a_t)\odot g_t \odot u_t,
\qquad a_t = \exp\!\big(-f_t\,\Delta t_t\big),
\label{eq:cfc}
\end{equation}
with $f_t=\mathrm{softplus}(z^f_t)$, $g_t=\tanh(z^g_t)$, $u_t=\sigma(z^u_t)$ and pre-activations
$z^{k}_t = W_k x_t + U_k h_{t-1} + b_k$. The dependency $z_t \leftarrow h_{t-1}$ is what makes the
computation sequential: each step needs a matrix--vector product that cannot start before the previous one
ends. Two observations motivate everything below. First, if a channel's gates were \emph{constant}, its
recurrence would be linear in $h$ and could be folded into an associative scan of depth
$O(\log L)$. Second, the gates are not constant --- but they are \emph{typically} concentrated on a small
subset of channels, so the state can be split into a hot part that keeps \eqref{eq:cfc} and a cold part that
is linearised around its mean trajectory.

That is the \piCfC{} design: choose a permutation $\pi$, run the first $(1-\rho)D$ channels as a parallel scan,
run the remaining $\rho D$ channels sequentially, and combine them through the recurrent tensor. The design
raises three questions that this report answers with measurements rather than assertions.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Is the permutation sound?} Yes, exactly --- but only bilaterally. Section~\ref{sec:iso}.
\item \textbf{Does it preserve what the network computes?} Partly, and the boundary is task complexity, not
      a constant in the paper. Sections~\ref{sec:equiv} and~\ref{sec:express}.
\item \textbf{Is it faster?} Yes on the GPU in the register-resident regime; on the CPU only in a
      well-identified large-shape regime. Sections~\ref{sec:kernels}--\ref{sec:bench}.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item A constructive \emph{bilateral} isomorphism for CfC layers (Theorem~\ref{thm:iso}) with a proof, a
      negative control, and an fp64/fp32 verification protocol that pins the residual to rounding
      ($1.0\times10^{-15}$/$4.6\times10^{-7}$).
\item A \emph{leakage-free} evaluation of six decoupling rules on three trained checkpoints, including
      two negative results that the first version of this work got wrong (Section~\ref{sec:bugs}).
\item A measured \emph{amplification law}: the trajectory error of a stable recurrence with effective
      decay $\abar$ grows as $(1-\abar^T)/(1-\abar)$ until saturation, verified inside a two-sided bound in
      13 of 15 cells.
\item A training-free \emph{ridge readout recalibration} (Gram accumulation in fp64, $24$--$433$~ms) that repairs
      most of the cold-linearisation loss on the two well-conditioned models ($R^2$ $0.216\to0.945$).
\item Hand-written Triton and AVX-512 kernels for the decoupled path \emph{and} for both sequential
      baselines --- including a single-launch kernel that runs the whole recurrence inside one program ---
      all gated to $<4.4\times10^{-7}$ relative $L_2$ at every width, with a measured map of where each
      wins, and an explicit statement that single-launch does not rescue a bandwidth-bound recurrence.
\item Two \emph{real-data} models (WikiText bytes, ten real video clips) that reproduce the ordering
      effect outside our own generators, with the closed-form recalibration's limits measured as well
      (Section~\ref{sec:real}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Decoupling rules.} For a checkpoint we compute, on a calibration batch of 128 sequences,
(a) the empirical state covariance $H = \mathbb{E}_t[h_th_t^\top]$ (cold-input rows only),
(b) the mean state $\bar h$, and (c) the mean linearised step Jacobian $J$. From these we form six rules:
\emph{pure-linear} ($\rho=0$), \emph{native-prefix} (hot set = first $\rho D$ channels), \emph{random-$\pi$}
(three seeds, mean $\pm$ std reported), \emph{variance-$\pi$} (hot = largest variance), and two of ours:
\emph{ours-jacobian-$\pi$}, which orders channels by $J$'s spectral participation (residual
$\|J_{:,i}-\bar j_i\mathbf{1}\|$ combined with the co-activation clustering of \code{src/ordering.py}), and
\emph{ours-jacobian-picard1}, which adds one Picard sweep on the cold half. All rules are applied to the
\emph{same} trained weights (the permutation families share no channel order: identity, five random orders of distinct seeds, variance, weight-norm, and two Jacobian-based), so the comparison is
about ordering, not about retraining.

\section{Isomorphism}\label{sec:iso}

\begin{theorem}[Bilateral channel isomorphism]\label{thm:iso}
Let $O$ be a permutation matrix acting on the \emph{channel} dimension, $W_k' = O^\top W_k$,
$b_k'=O^\top b_k$, $U_k' = O^\top U_kO$, $W_{\text{out}}' = W_{\text{out}}O$, and let $\Phi$ denote one CfC
step \eqref{eq:cfc} with the full (hot+cold) update. Then for every $x_t$, $\Delta t_t$ and initial state
$h_0$,
\[
\Phi'(h_0') = O^\top \Phi(h_0), \qquad h_0' = O^\top h_0,
\]
and consequently the $L$-step readout satisfies $y'_L = y_L$ exactly in exact arithmetic.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $h = Oh'$, i.e.\ $h' = O^\top h$, and use $O O^\top = I$. Each pre-activation is affine in
$x$ and $h$, so
\[
z_k = W_kx + U_kh + b_k = O\big(O^\top W_k x\big) + O\big(O^\top U_k O\big)h' + O\big(O^\top b_k\big)
    = O\big(W_k'x + U_k'h' + b_k'\big) = O\,z_k',
\]
i.e.\ $z'_k = O^\top z_k$ channel-wise (the input $x\in\R^{d_{\text{in}}}$ is untouched by $O$, which acts
only on the $D$-dimensional state and channel axis). The nonlinearities $\exp(\cdot)$, softplus, $\tanh$ and
$\sigma$ act element-wise, hence $a'=O^\top a$, $g'=O^\top g$, $u'=O^\top u$ and
$h'_t = a'\odot h'_{t-1} + (1-a')\odot g'\odot u' = O^\top h_t$ by induction. The readout is
$y_t = W_{\text{out}}'h'_t = W_{\text{out}}OO^\top h_t = W_{\text{out}}h_t$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is elementary, and that is the point: it licenses a \emph{compile-time} rewrite (fuse the
permutation into the weights, then forget it) rather than a runtime index permutation, and it explains
precisely why the one-sided variant fails. If only the input projection is permuted, then
$W_k' = O^\top W_k$ but $U_k' \neq O^\top U_kO$, and the recurrent term mixes the wrong channels; the failure is
$O(1)$, not a rounding effect.

\paragraph{Spectral structure.} Before asking whether $\rho$-decoupling is accurate, it is worth asking whether
there is anything to decouple. Table~\ref{tab:iso} is silent on that; the spectrum of $J$ is not. For A/B/C we find Gini
coefficients of the one-step nonlinear residual $\mathcal{J}_i$ of $0.82$/$0.92$/$0.74$ and a top-$25\%$
participation share of $0.90$/$0.98$/$0.83$: the residual is strongly concentrated in the top quarter of
channels (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}), which is what makes any decoupling worth attempting at all. But
concentrating the one-step residual is not sufficient for accuracy at small $\rho$: when the recurrent
transition sits at the edge of stability ($\rho_J\approx1$, below), the remaining cold residual is amplified
over long horizons by recurrent cross-channel coupling, which is the mechanism measured in
Section~\ref{sec:amp} and the reason Table~\ref{tab:p2a} is not flat at $\rho=1/16$. \begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../figures/fig_residual_spectrum.pdf}
\caption{Residual curve $R(\rho)$: the share of the mean step Jacobian's Frobenius mass kept by the hot set,
for the three checkpoints, against the uniform reference. The three curves differ mainly in Gini
($0.82$/$0.92$/$0.74$), i.e.\ in how unevenly the mass is spread --- all three sit at the edge of stability
($\rho_J\approx1$).}
\label{fig:spectrum}

The practical consequence, which the ordering experiments then quantify
(Section~\ref{sec:equiv}), is that the \emph{ordering} can only exploit structure that is already there:
permuting channels cannot create concentration, it can only place the channels the task actually uses into
the hot set. Mean spectral radius is $0.997/0.992/1.006$
(max $1.003/1.000/1.014$) --- the trained networks sit at the edge of stability, which is exactly the regime
where error amplification matters (Section~\ref{sec:amp}).

\paragraph{Reading the tables.} Three patterns are stable across models. (1)~Ordering matters only when the
hot set is large: below $\rho\approx0.25$ every rule is within noise of \emph{every other}, and all of them are
barely better than the pure-linear endpoint. (2)~At $\rho\ge0.375$ the jacobian rule separates from the native

result rather than a tuning problem, for the reason in Section~\ref{sec:amp}.

meaningful. At $\rho=0.25$ the chunked scan deviates from the sequential loop by $8.97\times10^{-8}$ (fp32)

(\code{results/phase2_scan_check.json}) --- both gates pass with four orders of margin.

\section{Spectral amplification}\label{sec:amp}

The error of a linearised step is \emph{not} the error of the trajectory. Writing
$e_t = h_t - \hat h_t$ and linearising around the exact trajectory gives the affine recursion
$e_t = A_t e_{t-1} + \eta_t$ with $\eta_t$ the one-step linearisation residual. If the per-step decay
$\|A_t\|$ averages to $\abar$, the two extreme accumulation models are
\begin{equation}
\|e_T\| \le \|\eta\|\,\frac{1-\abar^{T}}{1-\abar}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\|e_T\| \approx \|\eta\|\sqrt{\frac{1-\abar^{2T}}{1-\abar^{2}}},
\label{eq:gain}
\end{equation}
the coherent (all steps aligned) and incoherent ($\eta_t$ independent) bounds. A stable recurrence with
$\abar\approx0.99$ therefore multiplies a $1\%$ per-step defect by up to $100$.

Thirteen of fifteen cells satisfy
$\text{quad}\le\varepsilon_{\text{traj}}\le\text{lin}$; the two exceptions are the $\rho=0.5$ cells with the
smallest residual, where second-order (nonlinear) amplification dominates a first-order bound. The mechanism
explains the model ranking. C-largescale has damped multi-timescale modes, so its residual enters a
contracting system and error saturates gracefully; A-oscillator sits on a limit cycle
($\rho_J\approx1.0$, $\abar\approx0.94$), so a phase shift accumulates linearly with $T$ and no
$W_{\text{out}}$ trained on the exact trajectory can track it. Note also that $\varepsilon_{\text{step}}$
\emph{decreases} with $\rho$ while $\abar$ increases: a larger hot set injects less error per step but
accumulates it longer, which is why the measured error is flat in $\rho$ for A and B.

\paragraph{Training-free recalibration.} The readout $y_t = W_{\text{out}}h_t^\pi + b_{\text{out}}$ is linear in
the state, so after decoupling it can be refit in closed form. We accumulate
$A=\sum_t \tilde H_t^\top\tilde H_t$ and $r=\sum_t\tilde H_t^\top y_t$ over $N_{\text{cal}}=128$ calibration
sequences (streamed, never materialised) and solve the ridge system in fp64 with
$\lambda=10^{-3}\cdot\overline{\mathrm{diag}(A)}$. No gradient step is taken. On the host, the solve costs
$23.5$~ms for \code{B_parity}, $55.0$~ms for \code{A_oscillator}, and $402$--$433$~ms for
\code{C_largescale} whose accumulation is $65{,}536\times1024$; accumulating the Gram on the GPU and
sending only the $1024\times1024$ system to the host brings the end-to-end refit to
\textbf{1.6--24.4~ms} (\code{C_largescale}: Gram $1.7$--$6.8$~ms, host solve $14.9$--$19.6$~ms), which is
the number Table~\ref{tab:ridgegpu} reports. The ``$<50$~ms'' target therefore holds on the GPU path for all
three models and on the host only for the small ones; we report the host cost of the large model as a
missed target rather than quoting the best case (Tables~\ref{tab:ridge} and~\ref{tab:ridgegpu}).

The recalibration is the single most valuable component of the pipeline for the \emph{well-conditioned} models:
C-largescale goes from $0.216$ (pure-linear, $\rho=0$) to $0.945$, i.e.\ ninety percent of the full CfC's

solve on the host --- is therefore $1.6$--$24.4$~ms for all three checkpoints, which meets the ``$<50$~ms''

\section{Hybrid parallel kernels}\label{sec:kernels}

\paragraph{GPU (Triton 3.9, RTX 5070 Laptop, 8~GB).} The decoupled forward is four stages: (1) one batched GEMM
for the input pre-activations; (2) a Triton chunked Blelloch scan over the $(1-\rho)D$ cold channels, with the
$\beta$ term biased by the full mean state, $\bar h_\pi U_{k}[:n_{\text{cold}}]^\top$; (3) one batched GEMM for
the exogenous cold$\to$hot coupling, $U_{k}[\text{hot},\text{cold}]\,h^{\text{cold}}_{0:L-1}$; (4) a
register-resident recurrence over the $\rho D$ hot channels, where the three $d_{\text{hot}}^2$ tiles are held
in registers for all $L$ steps, so the sequential part never touches HBM. The Picard variant adds one hot$\to$cold
GEMM plus a second scan. Baselines are a PyTorch step loop (input projection hoisted out of the loop) and a
\emph{fused Triton sequential kernel} that keeps $h_{t-1}$ in registers but still pays $L$ launches.

\paragraph{Closing the $d_{\text{hot}}>64$ regime.} The register kernel above is capped at $d_{\text{hot}}\le64$
by three $d_{\text{hot}}^2$ fp32 tiles in shared memory; the first version of this work fell back to a
PyTorch step loop beyond that, which cost one kernel launch per step and collapsed the speedup to
$1.2$--$1.4\times$. \code{_hot_l2} replaces the fallback: one program per batch row keeps the whole
$L$-step loop \emph{inside} a single Triton kernel, holds $h$ in registers, and walks the three
$d_{\text{hot}}^2$ matrices in $32$-wide column tiles, gathering the matching state lanes from the register
vector itself. At $d_{\text{hot}}=512$ the three weight matrices total $3$~MiB, so they stay resident in the
$32$~MiB L2 for every step and every program and are never re-fetched from HBM; the only per-step traffic is
the $3d_{\text{hot}}$ gate pre-activations. The path is gated like every other one
(Table~\ref{tab:gate}) and extends the same kernel-launch amortisation to $64<d_{\text{hot}}\le512$, i.e.\ to
every configuration in the sweep except $D=2048$ at $\rho\ge1/2$.

\paragraph{A single-launch sequential baseline (and why it is not a fix).} Comparing a
single-kernel $\pi$-CfC against an $L$-step Python loop at $D\ge256$ would flatter us, so the
review's first objection is answered by building the missing baseline: \code{_seq_l2} runs the
\emph{entire} sequential CfC recurrence, for every $D\le2048$, inside \emph{one} Triton launch.
Each program owns one batch row, keeps $h$ in registers as a $B_D$-wide vector, and walks the
three $d^2$ matrices in $32$-wide column tiles, gathering the matching state lanes with
\code{tl.gather}; the input pre-activations are still one batched GEMM, exactly as in the loop
baseline. The kernel is gated at every $D$ (Table~\ref{tab:gate}) and clocked like every other
path. It is the fairest denominator we can construct, and the table reports the speedup against
$\min(\text{loop},\text{this kernel})$ rather than against the loop alone.

The result is a negative one that is worth stating: single-launch helps where the recurrence is
launch-bound and hurts where it is bandwidth-bound. At $D=256$ it is several times faster than
the loop; at $D=1024$ it is slower than the loop, because $B$ programs give $B$-way parallelism,
the recurrence uses no tensor cores, and three $D^2$ matrices (48~MB at $D=2048$) no longer fit
the 32~MB L2. That is the honest argument for decoupling: $\pi$-CfC does not win by amortising
launches, it wins by replacing $3D^2$ of recurrent matrices with $3(\rho D)^2$ and a parallel
scan --- a reduction that no amount of launch engineering can imitate.

\paragraph{Tensor cores.} Turning on TF32 for the batched GEMMs of the decoupled forward is
measured separately (\code{results/phase4_tf32.json}): it buys $1.01$--$1.02\times$ and moves the
trajectory error from $3.3\times10^{-7}$ to $2.0\times10^{-5}$, i.e.\ it costs a factor of $60$ in
accuracy to save $1$--$2\%$ of wall time, because the GEMMs are not what the run waits on. It is
\emph{not} used in any gated row:
TF32's 10-bit mantissa perturbs each step, and the recurrence multiplies its own error by a gain
of $G_\infty\approx17$ on these models, so the trajectory error lands far above the $10^{-5}$
budget of Table~\ref{tab:gate}. We report the TF32 timing as an upper bound on what tensor cores
would buy, and keep IEEE fp32 everywhere a number is claimed.

\paragraph{CPU (AVX-512, Ryzen 9 9955HX, 16 physical cores, \code{OMP_WAIT_POLICY=passive}).}
\code{kernels/avx512_picfc.cpp} exports \code{seq_cfc}, \code{cold_coeff}, \code{prefix_scan} and
\code{hot_recur}. The scan is work-optimal and two-pass: per-lane-chunk $P,Q$ triples in parallel, a serial scan
of the $T/64$ chunk summaries, then a carry fix-up. The gate design was itself an optimisation: computing the
cold coefficients in Python cost $3.7$~ms at $D{=}256$ because torch's CPU $\tanh/\exp$ are scalar
($\sim$3.3~ns each), so the coefficients moved into the kernel. A second round of the same lesson: driving the
four stages from Python costs $7.5$--$8.4$~ms at $D{=}256$, $L{=}256$ because every stage re-materialises
permuted views and pays a ctypes crossing, so \code{picfc_forward_fused} now executes cold coefficients,
scan, the exogenous coupling GEMM, the hot recurrence and the scatter \emph{inside} one C call
(Table~\ref{tab:cpu}).

\section{Real sequence streams}\label{sec:real}

Every model so far comes from a generator we wrote, and a reviewer is entitled to ask whether the
phenomenon survives contact with data we did not generate. Two additional models are trained on
real streams, with all hyper-parameters, code paths and gates unchanged:
Model~D (\emph{CfC-WikiText}) consumes real English text one byte at a time, with a $256$-way
one-hot input, an event timestep that doubles at sentence boundaries, and next-byte prediction as
the task; Model~E (\emph{CfC-SensorTrace}) consumes ten real CIF video clips ($4\,410$ frames of genuine
camera footage: surveillance, traffic, news, hand-held nature) as streams of $16\times16$ grey frames
at the clips' own frame step and predicts the next frame's $256$ pixel values, so sensor noise, global
illumination change and true scene motion enter the state at every step. Both use $D=512$ and a single
linear readout, i.e.\ the same architecture class as Models~A--C.

\paragraph{Three disjoint regions, and what each one is allowed to touch.} Every real stream is cut
into three contiguous, non-overlapping regions at $70/15/15\%$: the \emph{train} region fits the
exact base model ($\rho=1$, full BPTT) and is the only region used to fit a readout; the
\emph{calibration} region estimates the Jacobian scores $\mathcal{J}_i$, the mean state $\bar h$ used
by the reconstruction, and the $5$-epoch DAFT fine-tune; the \emph{test} region is touched exactly
once, by the metric, for every configuration including the exact model. For text the cut is temporal on
the byte stream; for video it is temporal \emph{inside every clip}, so each clip contributes to all
three regions (the content distribution is shared) while no frame, and therefore no window, is shared.
Windows never cross a region boundary, and the regulariser of the closed-form refit is selected on a
held-out slice of \emph{windows} rather than of rows, since rows inside one window are correlated.
Section~\ref{sec:bugs} records the leak that the previous version of this protocol hid.

\paragraph{What these two models do and do not show.} They show that the concentration of the step
Jacobian, the ordering effect and the recoverability of the decoupled fit are properties of
\emph{data with real temporal statistics}, not of our generators: the same ordering that wins on
Parity-Stream wins here, and the same recalibration recipe restores accuracy. They do not show that
$\pi$-CfC is competitive as a language model --- Model~D is a single-layer byte-level CfC with one
linear readout, evaluated on a $16$\,k-byte slice, and its BPD is reported to establish a
\emph{relative} ordering between selection rules, not to compete with any transformer. Both models
are trained once, with one seed, because the claim being tested is a mechanism, and the mechanism is
visible in the ordering of methods rather than in the third decimal of an absolute score.

\section{What went wrong first}\label{sec:bugs}

Every number above was re-measured after at least one of the following defects was found; they are listed
because each one changed a published conclusion.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
\item \textbf{Leakage in the ridge study} (metric: parity accuracy $0.848$ for a model that cannot do parity).
      The calibration split was the first half of the \emph{evaluation} batch. With an independently generated
      split, pure-linear scores $0.496$/$0.505$ after ridge, and the shuffled-target control $0.502$.
\item \textbf{Leakage in DAFT} (metric: $+0.62$ $R^2$ on A-oscillator). Fine-tuning used the evaluation batch;
      with fresh sequences the honest gain is $+0.32$.
\item \textbf{One-step lookahead} ($\varepsilon$ growing with $\rho$, $1.88$ relative error at
      $\rho=1$). The cold state fed to the hot gates was $h^{\text{cold}}_t$ instead of $h^{\text{cold}}_{t-1}$.
\item \textbf{Picard double counting} ($\sim10^3\times$ error): the cold refinement added
      $\bar h U^\top$ on top of the exact coupling term.
\item \textbf{Triton precision mismatch}: \code{tl.dot} defaults to tf32, which turned a feedback loop into
      $2.5\times10^{-1}$ relative error; \code{input_precision="ieee"} plus
      \code{allow_tf32=False} on the reference was also needed, since the reference itself deviated by
      $4\times10^{-5}$ under tf32 and masqueraded as kernel error.
\item \textbf{Triton assumes contiguous pointers}: passing \code{gf[:, :, :n_cold]} views produced silent
      garbage; every kernel argument is now \code{.contiguous()}.
\item \textbf{In-place hot update} in C++: channels updated before their dependents read $h_{t-1}$; fixed by
      computing all three gate vectors into temporaries first ($10$~ms $\to$ $0.6$~ms per call).
\item \textbf{Biased baselines}: a work-inefficient $O(T\log T)$ Hillis--Steele scan as the CPU baseline
      ($169$~ms versus $18$~ms for the work-optimal scan), and a PyTorch GPU loop that recomputed the input
      projection inside the loop.
\item \textbf{Wrong reduction axis in the tiled hot kernel} ($9$--$11\%$ relative error, i.e.\ plausible but
      wrong). The host passes $U^\top$, so a step computes $z_j=\sum_i h_i M_{ij}$; the first version reduced
      along the row tile and silently computed $M^\top h$ instead. Caught by the gate, not by the benchmark:
      the wrong version was also \emph{fast}.
\item \textbf{Hot-block compaction in the fused C entry} (error $10^{-2}$). The single-entry kernel handed
      \code{hot_recur} a pointer into the full matrix (\code{Uf + n_cold}) although that routine expects a
      dense $d_{\text{hot}}\times d_{\text{hot}}$ block with stride $d_{\text{hot}}$; the offset \emph{and}
      the stride are both wrong unless the hot-time-hot block is copied out first.
\item \textbf{Gate that could not run} (crash, not a wrong number). The numerical gate was parameterised over
      $D$ but the fused sequential baseline is compiled for $d\le64$; at $D=256$ the gate aborted on an
      assertion instead of skipping the row, which is why the gate now reports only the paths that exist at
      that shape and says so in the table caption.
\item \textbf{Permutation direction on the real streams.} Our ordering helper returns a \emph{cold prefix}, so
      building it with \code{argsort(..., descending=True)} put the reported hottest channels into the cold
      group and made the Jacobian ordering behave exactly like index order ($+0.94$ BPD instead of $+0.59$).
      The two real-data models are what caught it: a synthetic-only validation would have kept the wrong sign.
\item \textbf{``Better than exact'' on the real streams} (metric: DAFT reaching $1.462$ BPD where the
      exact model scores $2.882$, and a refit reaching $0.993$ $R^2$ where the exact model scores
      $0.975$). Two separate causes, both now impossible by construction. First, the split was made on
      clip boundaries, so the ``held-out'' clip was scored while the base model had been fitted on a
      neighbouring clip of the same scene, and the refit was evaluated on the very rows it was fitted
      on. Second, DAFT and the refit were compared against an exact model evaluated on a different
      region. The audit replaced both with the $70/15/15$ protocol above: DAFT now reaches $2.923$ BPD
      at $\rho=1/4$ against $2.882$ for the exact model, the refit reaches $0.851$ $R^2$ against
      $0.900$, and the pure-linear decoupled model --- which is what the refit is actually for --- is
      lifted from $-2.708$ to $0.831$ $R^2$. An approximation that beats the thing it approximates is a
      bug report, not a result, and every table in Section~\ref{sec:real} now carries an assertion that
      it cannot happen.
\item \textbf{Least squares is not a maximum-likelihood head.} The closed-form refit of the byte model's
      $256$-way softmax readout is a one-hot least-squares problem: it moves BPD from $3.24$ to $7.56$. A
      Newton/IRLS variant diverged even after whitening the features and using a damped step, so we report the
      negative result and restrict the recalibration claim to regression readouts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{The honest summary.} The permutation is exact and free; the accuracy it preserves depends on the
task's state complexity and, for stiff systems, on a cheap linear readout recalibration; the speedup depends
on whether the hot weights fit in registers. A deployment recipe follows: use $\rho=1/16\dots1/8$ for
state-tracking tasks, spend 5 epochs of DAFT on the hot block, refit the readout in closed form, and treat
$d_{\text{hot}}>64$ as a different engineering problem (shared memory or a two-level state), not as a tuning
knob. The engineering follow-up in this report largely closes that gap: a single-launch kernel that keeps the
state in registers and walks the three recurrent tiles through L2 removes the per-step Python overhead and
recovers most of the lost factor for $64<d_{\text{hot}}\le512$, which is why the recommended operating point
is $\rho\lesssim1/4$ rather than $\rho\le64/D$. For stiff oscillatory dynamics we recommend \emph{not} decoupling at all until the readout is
recalibrated, and we would not apply the method to a task whose state must survive an arbitrarily long gap
without a write (only the flip-flop class passes that test here).

\paragraph{Threats to validity.} Models A--C and the non-$TC^0$ suite are synthetic, while Models D and E
evaluate two real sequence streams at $D=512$ (WikiText bytes and ten real video clips); the real-stream
study has one seed per model, so its numbers establish an ordering of methods, not a variance-aware
leaderboard. $D\le2048$; the CPU comparison is against our own
AVX-512 sequential kernel, which is a strong but not a vendor-optimal baseline; the GPU picture is
register-limited by construction; the three checkpoints are single-seed, so the $\pm$ we report is batch noise,
not training noise.
